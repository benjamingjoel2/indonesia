Most people arrive in the Red Centre with a single image in mind: Uluru at sunset, glowing against a darkening sky. It is every bit as striking as the photographs suggest. What surprises first-time visitors is everything around it, from the domes of Kata Tjuta and the quiet of the desert at dawn to the depth of meaning this country holds for the Anangu, its Traditional Owners.

## Understanding where you are

Uluru-Kata Tjuta National Park is jointly managed by Anangu and Parks Australia, and it is listed by UNESCO for both its natural and cultural values. Climbing Uluru has not been permitted since October 2019, a decision made at the request of Anangu, for whom the rock is a place of deep significance. Some sites around the base are also culturally sensitive, and signs ask visitors not to photograph them. Following these requests is simple, and it changes the experience for the better.

The best introduction is time with an Aboriginal guide. Walking part of the base with someone who can explain the Tjukurpa, the law and stories connected to the land, turns rock formations and waterholes into places with meaning. The Cultural Centre near the base is also worth an unhurried hour before you begin exploring.

## How long to stay

Three nights is the minimum we recommend, and four is better. That allows for:

- Sunrise and sunset at Uluru on different days, from different viewing areas
- A guided walk around part of the base, including Mutitjulu Waterhole
- The Valley of the Winds or Walpa Gorge walk at Kata Tjuta
- A scenic flight by helicopter or light aircraft over both formations
- An evening meal outdoors, with time to look at the night sky

Shorter stays tend to feel rushed, because the best activities happen at the edges of the day. The middle hours, especially in the warmer months, are for resting in the shade.

## Choosing where to stay

Accommodation near Uluru is concentrated in Yulara, the resort township just outside the national park, with one exception that stands apart. Longitude 131° is a small camp of tented pavilions on a dune ridge with uninterrupted views to Uluru, and its all-inclusive stays include guided experiences, which suits first-time visitors well. For families or those wanting more space, there are good options within Yulara itself.

## Beyond Uluru

If you have more time, the wider Red Centre rewards it. Kings Canyon, about three hours' drive away in Watarrka National Park, has a rim walk with views over sheer sandstone walls. Further east, the West MacDonnell Ranges near Alice Springs are cut by gorges and permanent waterholes, and the region has a strong tradition of Aboriginal art. We often link these by helicopter or private charter to avoid long days on the road.

## When to go

The most comfortable months are April to September, when days are mild and nights cool. June and July can bring frosty mornings, so pack a warm layer for sunrise. From November to March, temperatures commonly exceed 40°C; travel is still possible, but walks begin very early, and some trails close in the heat of the day.

## Practical notes

Ayers Rock Airport (Connellan) has direct flights from Sydney, Melbourne and Cairns, among others, and is a short transfer from Yulara and Longitude 131°. A national park pass is required, and we include it in your arrangements. Bring a fly net for the warmer months, sturdy walking shoes and a hat with a brim. Above all, leave some time with nothing planned; the desert is at its most powerful when you simply sit with it.
