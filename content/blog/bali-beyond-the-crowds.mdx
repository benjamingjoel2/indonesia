Most visitors to Bali spend their time in a fairly small triangle: the beach towns of the south, Canggu on the west coast and central Ubud. These places are popular because they are easy and lively, and they have some very good hotels and restaurants. But traffic between them can be slow, and the most photographed spots now come with queues.

The good news is that the rest of the island is close at hand. Bali is roughly 150 kilometres from east to west, and two hours in a car can take you somewhere that feels entirely different.

## Sidemen and East Bali

The Sidemen valley sits in the shadow of Mount Agung, Bali's highest and most sacred volcano. It is a landscape of terraced rice, small temples and villages known for weaving **songket**, a cloth threaded with gold. Accommodation is mostly small lodges and villas, and the pace is slow.

Further east, the coast around Amed and Tulamben has black sand beaches, calm morning water and good snorkelling, including the wreck of the USAT Liberty just offshore. Nearby, the water palace at Tirta Gangga is quieter early in the day.

## The central highlands

The lakes of Bratan, Buyan and Tamblingan sit in an old volcanic caldera in the centre of the island. At around 1,200 metres, the air is cool, the mornings are often misty and the forest is dense. The lakeside temple of Ulun Danu Beratan is well known, but the paths around Lake Tamblingan, where small shrines sit among the trees, see very few visitors.

The nearby hills around Munduk are planted with coffee, cloves and fruit. It is a lovely area for walking, with waterfalls and village guesthouses along the way.

## Jatiluwih and the west

The rice terraces of **Jatiluwih** are part of a UNESCO World Heritage listing that recognises Bali's subak, the cooperative irrigation system that has shared water between farmers for around a thousand years. They are much larger and calmer than the terraces near Ubud, and several walking trails cross them.

Further west, West Bali National Park covers forest, savannah and mangrove. Menjangan Island, just offshore, has some of the island's best snorkelling and diving walls, and the area remains very quiet.

## The north coast

Lovina and the coast around it are sleepier than the south. The sea is calm, the sand is dark, and you are close to hot springs, Buddhist monasteries and vineyards that supply Bali's small wine industry. It is a good stop on the way to or from the ferry to Java.

## How to plan it

You do not need to give up the familiar parts of Bali to see these regions. We often suggest:

- Two or three nights in Ubud for culture and food.
- Two or three nights in Sidemen or the central highlands for walking and quiet.
- A few nights in East or North Bali if you want the sea without the crowds.
- A final stretch in Uluwatu or Sanur, close to the airport, before you fly home.

A few practical tips help, too. Start sightseeing early, before 8am, when temples are cool and quiet. Ask your guide about local ceremonies, which are often open to respectful visitors in a sarong and sash. Travel with a private driver rather than relying on ride-hailing apps, which are restricted in some areas.

## Respect matters

The quieter parts of Bali are quieter partly because they are working villages, not attractions. Dress modestly in temples, ask before taking photographs of people and ceremonies, and buy from local makers where you can. It makes for a better trip, and it keeps these places the way they are.
